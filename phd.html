Motivation letter

Pakistan is one of the places where the planet’s changes are easiest to see and hardest to live with. Its rivers begin in the glaciers of the Karakoram, Hindu Kush and Himalaya; its plains depend on the Indus and the monsoon; its cities are growing faster than they can be planned. In 2022 floods affected some 33 million people and caused damage and losses estimated at more than US$30 billion. Glacier lakes threaten mountain valleys, droughts return to Sindh and Balochistan, groundwater is falling under the farms of Punjab, forests keep shrinking, and brick kilns and traffic fill the air of the cities. These are, at heart, spatial problems. They need to be seen from above, measured over time and explained in a way that decision-makers can act on. That is why I chose geospatial science, and why I want to pursue a PhD.

I fell in love with this field in my first year of a BS in Geo-informatics at PMAS Arid Agriculture University, Rawalpindi, the first time I watched a satellite image turn into a map of something real: a river that had moved, a field that had dried out, a town that had spread. I still find that moment exciting. I worked to deserve it: I topped semester examinations several times and received the Vice-Chancellor’s Talent Scholarship on each occasion, received the Prime Minister’s Laptop Award for academic merit, and graduated in 2022 as Silver Medalist, ranked second of 45 with a CGPA of 3.81. Throughout my degree I paid for my own studies, first through medical transcription and then through more than 376 independent GIS projects for international clients, and I completed a 360-hour GIS Surveyor programme with Grade A+.

Work then showed me what geospatial data means for people. As GIS Manager at Changaiz Enterprises (2022–2025) I ran drone and DGPS surveys and terrain models for large land projects, where a misplaced boundary becomes a family’s dispute. As GIS & Remote Sensing Specialist at Geomapping Solutions since 2025, I have produced 1 m terrain models from satellite stereo pairs and flood-hazard maps for hill torrents where more than three million people live in the exposed areas. There I saw that the edge of a modelled flood decides who is counted as at risk, and that a map is only as useful as the confidence we can place in it.

My MS in Remote Sensing & GIS (2024–2026, CGPA 3.96, again with the Vice-Chancellor’s Talent Scholarship for topping semester examinations) gave me my first full research project. I measured 25 years of glacier change in the Hunza River Watershed, building my own ASTER stereo DEMs from raw data for 2000, 2015 and 2025 and finding a basin-wide mass loss of −0.34 ± 0.15 m water equivalent per year, ice that feeds the Indus for millions of people downstream. Alongside my studies I co-authored three peer-reviewed papers with colleagues in Nigeria and Ethiopia, one as first author, and I am preparing six more on glaciers, crops, drought, earth tremors and air-pollution exposure. My studies changed how I see my own country: the same tools that describe a glacier can warn a valley, guide a dam, protect a harvest or show which schools breathe the dirtiest air.

A PhD is the natural next step because the questions I now care about cannot be answered by applying existing methods alone. I want to work broadly in Earth observation for water, hazards, land and climate: combining optical, radar, hyperspectral and terrain data with hydrological and environmental models, using machine learning on long time series, and quantifying uncertainty so that satellite products can support decisions with known reliability. I am open to the specific focus of the group I join, and I am used to learning new sensors, regions and methods quickly.

I would bring the ability to carry a project independently from raw imagery and field data to validated results, reproducible code in Python and Google Earth Engine, and publication-quality maps; experience of international collaboration through the Ban Ki-moon Centre Green Jobs mentorship, the Climate Change AI Summer School and the URISA mentoring network; and the habit of explaining geospatial science to a LinkedIn community of more than 16,000 professionals. In the long term I want to return this knowledge to regions like my own, where the need for reliable Earth-observation evidence is greatest and the capacity to produce it is still growing, through research, teaching and partnerships with the institutions that manage water, land and risk.

Thank you for considering my application. I would be glad to discuss how my background can contribute to your research.

— Hamza Sadaqat
